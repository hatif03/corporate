# Building Corporate, part 1: why a company of agents

*This is part 1 of a four-part series on building Corporate, a multi-agent web app for Google's All Things Agentic hackathon (track: The Fortified Enterprise Fleet, deadline 2026-08-31). This series is written for the hackathon submission — it's the full, unfiltered story: what we built, what we tried and threw away, and why, with no detail smoothed over for narrative convenience.*

## Accreditation, up front

Before anything else: Corporate's office-floor UI — the pixel art design system, the title bar, the roster strip, the layout skeleton of scene-plus-dashboard — is adapted from [`chaitanyagiri/munder-difflin`](https://github.com/chaitanyagiri/munder-difflin), an MIT-licensed reference app, at our own explicit request to "copy everything, to the last detail." We fetched the source directly via `gh api`, not from screenshots or assumptions, and ported large parts of it close to verbatim: the complete design-token set (colors, type scale, spacing, shadows), the theme-toggle module, the pixel-art button/badge/panel components, and the title-bar → office-floor → roster-strip structural skeleton.

**What we did *not* carry over, deliberately**: the reference app is a fundamentally different product — a downloadable, local CLI-agent harness with paid tiers, a "Founders' Wall" payment flow, and a platform-download picker. None of that applies to Corporate, a hosted multi-tenant web app with no download and no pricing. We also didn't take its tileset art (LimeZu "Modern Interiors," separately licensed, not covered by the reference's own MIT grant on its *code*) — Corporate uses its own [Kenney "1-Bit Pack"](https://kenney.nl/assets/1-bit-pack) tileset (CC0, public domain), rearranged into our own 3×3 room-and-corridor layout. And we didn't take its content: "The Office" character names, personas, dialogue, or the "Munder Difflin" brand itself — trademark-adjacent parody content specific to that project's own identity. Corporate has its own name, its own original agent personas, and its own department roster. Full attribution detail, including every individual component ported and every one explicitly left out, lives in `THIRD_PARTY_SKILLS.md` at the repo root — read that for the complete list, this is the summary.

One more credit while we're here: several department prompts include a short excerpt adapted from a curated, permissively-licensed prompt-engineering skill (financial analysis, chaos engineering, storybrand messaging, and others) — again, all MIT-licensed, all attributed by name and source in `THIRD_PARTY_SKILLS.md`, with only a short adapted excerpt used in each case, not the full skill.

## The shape question, before any code

The Fortified Enterprise Fleet track's own description is specific: "scalable institutional agent networks" with real components for discovery/lifecycle, execution/state management, security/governance, and telemetry, emphasizing "production compliance and cross-department deployment." That phrase — *cross-department* — is doing real work. It's not asking for one clever agent; it's asking for an organization.

So before writing a line of application code, the first real decisions were about shape, not features:

**Hosted, not desktop.** An early direction modeled Corporate as a local app that spawns agent processes on the user's own machine, with a thin cloud-sync layer bolted on just to technically touch Google Cloud. We rejected this outright. The hackathon needs a real hosted URL and load-bearing Google Cloud usage — Firestore, Pub/Sub, and Cloud Run doing the actual work, not sitting off to the side as a sync target. A "desktop for local dev, cloud for the submission" hybrid was also on the table briefly; rejected as duplicated effort with no feature it would actually unlock.

**One language, one framework, for the whole backend.** Each department could, in principle, pick whatever language and agent framework fit its own problem best — a fraud-detection pipeline in one stack, a customer-support classifier in another. We rejected this too. A coherent, single stack (Python, FastAPI, Google ADK 2.7.1) scores better against "Architectural Discipline" than a patchwork of five different runtimes would, and it means one team (or one contributor, or one Claude Code session picking this project back up) can actually read and reason about the whole system, not just their own corner of it.

**Firestore for state, Pub/Sub for messaging — not Firestore doing both.** A pure-Firestore design, where each agent polls its own "inbox" collection for new messages, was seriously considered. We rejected it: polling doesn't fit Cloud Run's request-driven scaling model — you either waste cost keeping instances warm to poll frequently, or you accept real latency between a message landing and an agent noticing it. Pub/Sub push, one topic (`agent-bus`) with one filtered subscription per agent, won instead. And within that: whether an agent's own judgment should decide if a reply is "owed," or whether that should be mechanical. We chose mechanical — `publish_message()` is the one function that increments a hop counter and derives `requires_reply` from the message's `act` field, and a hard cap (`hops > 12`) kills a runaway conversation and flags it for a human, no LLM ever gets a vote in whether this particular safety mechanism fires.

**A2A at the boundary, never internally.** ADK ships first-class A2A (Agent2Agent protocol) support, and it would technically be possible to route all internal CEO-to-department messaging over it. We rejected that specifically — there's no external, cross-vendor, opaque-agent problem to solve *inside* the company, and A2A's request/response shape is a worse fit for Cloud Run's push/fan-out model than Pub/Sub already is for that internal traffic. But we didn't drop A2A either: Sales & CRM is exposed as a real, standalone A2A server, its own Cloud Run service with its own `/.well-known/agent-card.json`, for genuinely external callers. That's the actual "Fortified Enterprise Fleet" trust-boundary story — a clear, deliberate seam between internal machinery and the outside world, not a protocol adopted everywhere just because it exists.

**One contract, not nine bespoke integrations.** The alternative to a shared department contract is each department wiring its own Firestore reads, its own Pub/Sub publish calls, its own audit logging, as needed. We rejected that as exactly the "everyone reinvents their own plumbing" failure mode a shared contract exists to prevent. Every department is a Python package exporting a `DepartmentSpec` and one function, `on_task_received(org_id, task) -> TaskResult` — the *only* entrypoint the platform ever calls, wrapped automatically in `@audited_task` for hash-chained audit logging and failure containment. A new department gets idempotent dispatch, tamper-evident logging, and "never crash, always reply" failure handling for free, on day one, with zero of its own code.

## What this bought us later

Every one of those five decisions reads, in hindsight, like an obvious call. They didn't feel obvious in the moment — the polyglot-department idea and the A2A-everywhere idea both had real, articulable arguments in their favor. What made the eventual call tractable wasn't confidence, it was writing down the alternatives that lost and *why*, in an ADR, before moving on. Twenty ADRs later (the count as of this writing), that discipline is the reason a three-days-to-deadline audit pass — covered in part 4 of this series — was a tractable few days of focused work instead of a panic. You can't safely re-derive whether a decision still holds if you never wrote down why it was made.
